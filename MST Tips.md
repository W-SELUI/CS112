## Q1 — Ethical Issue Using Ethical Interconnectedness

**What ethical interconnectedness means:** This concept explains that ethical issues don't exist in isolation — the actions of one person or group create effects that spread outward and impact multiple other people, groups, and institutions. It's about tracing the chain reaction of an action.

**How the sample answers it:**

- First, they name the specific issue (sale and storage of unsafe food — spoiled meat, pests, poor cleaning practices).
- Then they explain the theory in one sentence.
- Then they trace WHO is affected: consumers → businesses (industry reputation) → the Ministry of Health (resources for enforcement).
- They close with a summary sentence stating the issue "negatively impacts consumers, businesses, and the wider community."

**Full example (this is the level of detail/format you want):**

> "One ethical issue in the article is the sale and storage of unsafe food. Health inspectors found spoiled meat, pests such as cockroaches and weevils, and poor cleaning practices in some businesses. According to ethical interconnectedness, the actions of one group affect many others. When businesses fail to maintain food safety, consumers can become sick and lose trust in food establishments. Other businesses may also suffer because the reputation of the industry is damaged. The Ministry of Health must then spend time and resources enforcing regulations. Therefore, the actions of these businesses negatively impact consumers, businesses, and the wider community."

That's the exact shape to copy: **name the issue with detail from the article → define the concept → list 3 affected parties with a reason for each → summary sentence.**

## Q2 — Virtue Ethics

**What virtue ethics means:** This theory judges whether someone is ethical based on their **character traits (virtues)**, not just their actions or the outcome. A virtuous person/institution acts from good character — honesty, responsibility, integrity, compassion. A person lacking these traits shows poor moral character even if no one gets caught.

**How the sample answers it:**

- Names ONE group → gives them a specific virtue → explains what they did that proves it.
- Names a SECOND group → gives them a vice (or lack of virtue) → explains what they did that proves it.
- Closes with a one-line theory statement tying both together.

**Full example:**

> "The Ministry of Health and Medical Services demonstrates the virtue of responsibility. It carried out inspections and enforced food safety laws to protect consumers from health risks. This shows good moral character and concern for public wellbeing. The non-compliant businesses showed a lack of integrity. By allowing unsafe food, pests, and poor hygiene conditions, they failed to act honestly and responsibly towards customers. Their actions reflect poor moral character. According to virtue ethics, good behavior comes from good character traits. The Ministry displayed positive virtues, while the offending businesses failed to do so."

Virtues bank: responsibility, integrity, honesty, care/compassion, courage, fairness. Vice bank (opposite of the above): negligence, dishonesty, greed, indifference, recklessness.

## Q3 — Wrongdoing + Deontology

**What deontology means:** Founded by Kant. It judges actions as right or wrong based on whether **duty and moral rules** were followed — not the outcome. Even if breaking a rule accidentally leads to a good result, deontology still says it was wrong, because duty comes first.

**How the sample answers it:**

- (2 marks) States the wrongdoing plainly, in one sentence, tied to the article.
- (3 marks) Names TWO deontological principles, one at a time, each explained with a sentence connecting it back to the wrongdoing.

**Full example:**

> "The wrongdoing in the article is that some businesses failed to comply with food safety regulations and exposed consumers to potential health risks. From a deontological perspective, the first principle is duty. Businesses have a duty to follow food safety rules and provide safe products to consumers. They failed to meet this duty. The second principle is respect for persons. Customers have a right to safe food and should not be put at risk. By selling unsafe food, the businesses failed to respect their customers. Therefore, their actions are unethical according to deontology."

Principle bank: duty, respect for persons (don't treat people as a means to profit), obeying moral rules/laws, consumer rights, universalizability (would it be okay if everyone did this?).

## Q4 — Utilitarian Response

**What utilitarianism means:** Founded by Bentham/Mill. It judges an action as right if it produces **the greatest good/happiness for the greatest number** of people, and wrong if it causes more harm than benefit. Unlike deontology, it's 100% about the outcome, not the rule.

**How the sample answers it:**

- (2 marks) States what a utilitarian would think/support in this specific situation, tied to article.
- (3 marks) Names TWO utilitarian principles, each explained.

**Full example:**

> "A utilitarian would support the Ministry's decision to fine and close businesses that violated food safety regulations because these actions protect the public from harm. One utilitarian principle is the greatest good for the greatest number. The enforcement of food safety laws benefits many consumers by reducing health risks. Another principle is maximizing overall happiness and wellbeing. Safe food standards improve public health and create trust in businesses, producing positive outcomes for society. Therefore, a utilitarian would agree with the Ministry's actions."

Principle bank: greatest good for the greatest number, maximizing happiness/wellbeing, minimizing harm, increasing overall welfare.

## Q5 — Governance

**What "governance" means here:** The systems/approaches used to manage and control a situation, and the standards (principles) that those in power are supposed to uphold.

**How the sample answers it:**

- (2 marks) Names TWO governance approaches shown in the article — usually tied to what the enforcing body (Ministry) did.
- (3 marks) Names TWO governance principles that were compromised (broken) by the wrongdoers, each explained.

**Full example:**

> "Regulatory governance is shown through inspections, fines, and closure orders used by the Ministry. Protective governance is shown through efforts to protect public health and ensure food safety standards are followed. Accountability was compromised because some businesses did not properly maintain food safety standards. Responsibility was compromised because businesses failed to provide safe food and hygienic conditions for customers. Therefore, while the Ministry demonstrated good governance, the offending businesses failed to uphold important governance principles."

Approach bank: regulatory governance (laws, fines, inspections), protective governance (protecting public welfare). Principle bank: accountability, responsibility, transparency, rule of law.

ANOTHER SCENARIO

**Scenario for reference:** A student reported a sexual assault to school/institution authorities. The institution delayed action, didn't report it to police, and tried to keep it quiet to protect its reputation. The perpetrator faced no immediate consequences and the victim was not given proper support.

---

## Q1 — Ethical Interconnectedness

> "One ethical issue in the article is the institution's failure to act on a report of sexual assault. Rather than reporting the incident to police and supporting the victim, the institution delayed action and attempted to handle the matter quietly. According to ethical interconnectedness, this failure does not only affect the victim — it ripples outward. The victim is directly harmed, both by the assault itself and by the institution's failure to protect and support her afterward. Other students are indirectly affected, as they lose trust that the institution will protect them if something similar happens. The wider community loses confidence in the institution's ability to handle serious matters responsibly, and law enforcement's ability to investigate is delayed or compromised. This shows how one institution's failure to act ethically created harm far beyond the immediate incident."

## Q2 — Virtue Ethics

> "The victim (or a whistleblower/staff member who reported it) demonstrated the virtue of courage by coming forward despite the risk of not being believed or supported. This reflects strength of character in a difficult situation. In contrast, the institution demonstrated a lack of integrity and honesty. By prioritizing its reputation over the wellbeing and safety of the victim, and by not reporting the matter to the proper authorities, it acted dishonestly and evasively. According to virtue ethics, actions reveal character — the victim's/reporter's actions reflect courage, while the institution's response reflects cowardice and dishonesty."

## Q3 — Wrongdoing + Deontology

> "The wrongdoing in the article is that the institution failed to report a serious incident to the appropriate authorities and did not take proper action to protect the victim. From a deontological perspective, the first principle is duty — institutions have a moral and legal duty to report serious crimes and protect those in their care, and this duty was not fulfilled. The second principle is respect for persons — by prioritizing its own reputation over the victim's wellbeing, the institution failed to treat her as a person deserving of protection and dignity, instead treating the situation as a problem to be managed. Therefore, the institution's actions are unethical according to deontology, regardless of what outcome it hoped to achieve."

## Q4 — Utilitarian Response

> "A utilitarian would strongly oppose the institution's decision to delay action and avoid involving the police, because this choice caused greater harm than good. One utilitarian principle is minimizing harm — by not acting quickly, the institution allowed continued risk to the victim and potentially to others. Another principle is the greatest good for the greatest number — properly reporting and addressing the incident would have protected the victim, deterred future harm to other students, and preserved trust in the institution, benefiting far more people than staying silent did. Therefore, a utilitarian would judge the institution's response as ethically wrong because it failed to produce the best overall outcome."

## Q5 — Governance

> "Two approaches of governance are relevant here. Protective governance should have been used to safeguard the victim and prevent further harm, but was largely absent. Regulatory governance — following mandatory reporting laws and procedures — was also not properly followed. Two governance principles were compromised: accountability, because the institution did not take ownership of its failure to respond appropriately, and transparency, because it attempted to handle the matter privately instead of following proper legal and institutional procedures. This shows a serious breakdown in governance, where protecting reputation was placed above protecting people."
